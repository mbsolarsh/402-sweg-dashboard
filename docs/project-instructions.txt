What you will learn: how to research users in a real organization, turn a vague goal into measurable KPIs, design a data dashboard people can read at a glance, test it, and run agile sprints with a real client.
The client and the problem
402 SWEG wants a central dashboard that shows leadership how its software teams are performing and how that performance changes over time. Here is how the Product Owner describes the project:
To provide visibility to leadership and track performance trends, the 402 Software Engineering Group (SWEG) is interested in developing and maintaining a central dashboard for software development metrics. The dashboard should provide quick-glance data on measurable benchmarks and key performance indicators (KPIs) for project managers and team leaders to assess the team performance (team-level, not individual level). Ultimately, the data in the dashboard should reveal performance trends and inform changes in productivity/efficiency.
Our main issue on this project has been developing the KPIs and other metrics that might be useful. For example, we can easily track the number of software lines that a developer produced, but this metric does not correlate at all to the quality, efficiency, or overall usefulness of the software. Some potential metrics that we can produce are: burndown charts, CI/CD pipeline pass/fail rates, and unit test code coverage percentages.
What this means for your team
	•	The metrics are the hardest part. Choosing the right KPIs is the Product Owner's main problem. A beautiful dashboard showing the wrong numbers will not win.
	•	Team level only. The dashboard measures teams, never individual developers. Do not design rankings, leaderboards, or per-person views.
	•	Avoid vanity metrics. Lines of code is the Product Owner's own example of a number that is easy to count but says nothing about quality or usefulness. Every KPI you propose must explain what decision it helps someone make.
	•	Quick glance, then trends. Users should understand the current state in seconds, then be able to look at trends over time.
	•	Two audiences. Project managers and team leaders use the dashboard to assess their teams; leadership uses it for visibility across teams.

Research and metrics guidance
Shared survey
	•	Each team submits 8 to 10 draft questions by Sun Nov 1. The instructor merges them into one survey of about 15 questions, and the Product Owner approves and distributes it to project managers and team leaders.
	•	One survey spares the client from answering three. The survey closes Thu Nov 12, and every team receives the same anonymized responses on Fri Nov 13.
	•	Good topics: what decisions users make about their teams, what information they lack today, which metrics they already watch, how often they would check a dashboard, what devices they use, and concerns about how metrics might be misused.
Comparable dashboards review
Review 3 existing engineering-metrics dashboards. Compare them on the same criteria: which metrics they show, how they show trends, how fast you can read them, and what you would borrow or avoid.
Building the KPI framework
Start from established frameworks instead of inventing metrics from scratch. Research at least these two:
	•	DORA metrics: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, and time to restore service.
	•	SPACE framework: satisfaction and well-being, performance, activity, communication and collaboration, and efficiency and flow.
Also consider the metrics the Product Owner named: burndown charts, CI/CD pipeline pass and fail rates, and unit test code coverage. Then narrow your 12 to 15 candidates down to 6 to 10 recommended KPIs. Document each one in a table with these columns:
Column
What to write
KPI
Name and one-line definition
Formula
How it is calculated, with units
Data source
Where the data would come from (for example, Trello or the CI/CD pipeline)
Question it answers
The decision it helps a team leader or manager make
Benchmark or target
What "good" looks like and where that comes from
Risk of gaming
How the number could be inflated without real improvement, and how you would guard against it

Balance your set across speed, quality, stability, and team health. A team that ships fast but breaks things should not look good on your dashboard.
Prototype requirements
Your Figma prototype must fully satisfy the Product Owner's stated requirements. It is not a set of sample screens: it must work as a complete dashboard.
	•	Fully functional. Every view, filter, menu, and drill-down works. A user can explore the whole dashboard without hitting dead ends.
	•	All selected KPIs. Every KPI in your framework appears in the prototype, with realistic sample data, its current value, and its trend over time.
	•	Quick glance, then trends. Leadership and team leaders see the current status in seconds and can drill down into trends by team and time period.
	•	Team level only. No individual developer data, rankings, or leaderboards.
	•	Accessible. Text and chart colors meet WCAG 2.2 AA contrast, meaning never relies on color alone, charts have clear labels and legends, and text is readable at normal size.
	•	User-friendly. Clear labels in the users' language, a consistent layout, and short explanations of what each KPI means. Usability testing must show that users can answer their key questions quickly.

Guerrilla usability testing
	•	Run 5 sessions per team, 10 to 15 minutes each, with 3 short tasks drawn from your personas.
	•	Real users are best: ask the instructor whether the Product Owner can arrange 1 or 2 project managers or team leaders. Otherwise, test with people who have project management or software team experience.
	•	Test whether users can answer real questions quickly, such as: "Is this team's quality getting better or worse?"
